Design comparison

What changes when size becomes the message?

In both views, the United States and China stand out first; together they account for 45.6% of the top-50 total. The choropleth also makes geographically large countries such as Russia, Canada, Australia, and Brazil visually prominent even when their color is only midrange. I used a logarithmic color scale because the United States is more than one hundred times the value of the smallest economy in the file. A linear scale would compress most countries into nearly the same pale color. The choropleth is therefore best for locating GDP values, recognizing regional patterns, and relating the data to familiar borders and neighbors.

The cartogram changes that reading. Its circle areas are proportional to GDP, so the United States and China dominate, followed by Germany, Japan, India, and the United Kingdom. Compact economies such as Germany, South Korea, the Netherlands, Singapore, and Hong Kong become much larger relative to their geographic footprints. Russia, Canada, Australia, and Kazakhstan shrink sharply. This makes the cartogram better for comparing economic magnitude and noticing how concentrated the top-50 total is. The cost is geographic detail: borders, coastlines, country shapes, and exact adjacency disappear, and overlapping regions must be displaced. Switching from color to area makes economic inequality feel more immediate, but linked highlighting is necessary to reconnect the distorted circles to recognizable places.
